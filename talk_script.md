## Slide 3. Adults: Event processing at two levels

1. Question: does how a language encodes events align with how speakers process them? English and Mandarin speakers saw 44 matched videos of the same object being cut or blended; cutting has a clear endpoint, blending does not.
2. The contribution is separating two levels of encoding. At the lexical level, English marks the object with quantized nouns (68% vs. 13%) and Mandarin marks the result with resultatives, mostly for blending (44% vs. 17%). At the predicate level, English is more telic for cutting (79% vs. 51%), Mandarin for blending.
3. Each level has its own processing measure, and the effect appears where that level’s contrast is. Lexical level and interruption detection: in blending, English speakers caught early interruptions better, Mandarin speakers late ones (p = .040). Predicate level and boundary tapping: in cutting, Mandarin speakers placed the end 862 ms later (p = .005), with no difference at onset.
4. Experiments 4 and 5 added brief lexical and telicity primes. Group differences recurred, but primes did not shift either level (Bayes factor about 1000 for the null). So the link is stable but not created by a one-off cue, which motivates looking at learning and development.

## Slide 4. Mandarin Children’s action–result language

1. Children often reject “she peeled the lychee” when the lychee is not open, more than adults do. The question is why.
2. We filmed 10 action pairs that share an object and a result, each with the result attained or not; 32 four-year-olds, 32 five-year-olds and 40 adults per experiment judged and described them.
3. Our proposal: how strongly a verb implies its result depends on the action–object–result relation, not only on the verb. Peeling very likely opens a lychee (rated 93); squeezing less so (65). Stronger association should mean more rejection when the result is absent.
4. [Main result here, or: data are collected and analysis is in progress.] This study gives me experience testing children in kindergartens and materials I can reuse.

## Slide 5. Children: A pragmatic account of superordinates in Mandarin

1. This sole-authored paper, at minor revision in Language and Cognition, tests the pragmatic-informativeness account of Choe and Papafragou: superordinate nouns like “animal” are under-informative, so speakers use them in contexts that signal generality, such as quantifiers, wh-phrases, “kind of” and “another”. The account was built on English; Mandarin is a strong test because it lacks obligatory plural marking.
2. Study 1 replicates their corpus analysis in 11 Mandarin CHILDES corpora: do these contexts separate superordinate from basic-level nouns in Mandarin child-directed speech?
3. They do. All four Mandarin-available contexts were associated with superordinates (odds ratios 2.1 to 8.2), robust to overlapping cues, and a classifier recovered semantic level from the contexts (AUC = .73). So the account replicates beyond English.
4. Study 2 asks whether a word’s exposure to these cues predicts its age of acquisition, in Mandarin and English. Corpus frequency did; cue exposure added little. The cues are present in the input, but whether children use them needs experiments.

## Slide 6. Future research: three directions

1. All three directions ask how event relations, an action changing an object’s state, are learned, and each keeps the two levels from my thesis: lexical cues map onto event phases, predicate telicity onto endpoints. They use new hands-only event pairs, such as tearing paper in half versus crumpling it, normed with adults first; this also addresses the thesis limit of only cutting and blending.
2. Direction 1, when children learn event relations: Mandarin- and English-speaking children aged 3–6 describe the events, which gives each child’s lexical encoding (quantized nouns, resultatives) and predicate encoding (telic descriptions). They also do child versions of my two tasks: press when the video “hiccups” for phases, press when it is “done” for endpoints. Prediction: a child’s lexical encoding predicts phase sensitivity but not endpoints, and predicate encoding predicts endpoints but not phases. A 6-month follow-up asks whether language change comes first.
3. Direction 2, whether language helps children abstract relations: brief primes did not work in the thesis, so children get three training sessions with either lexical cues (一颗橙子, 切成两块) or predicate cues (切开 vs. 切一切), and are tested on both tasks. Prediction: lexical training shifts phase sensitivity and predicate training shifts endpoints, and the shift transfers to new events, for example from tearing to breaking, which would show they learned a relation, not a word.
4. Direction 3, whether language models capture these relations: following Rognan et al. (CogSci 2026), I treat video-language models as participants on my own videos. They describe the events in English and Mandarin, coded exactly like the human data, and judge frame by frame whether the object has changed, which gives phase and endpoint measures comparable to human data. Because the video is fixed, I can change only the language of the prompt: lexical prompts should shift phase judgments and predicate prompts endpoints. This is the clean causal test that brief primes could not give me in people, and comparing models with children shows what hands-on experience adds.
